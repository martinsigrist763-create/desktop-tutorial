AI, judgement and the next societal transition

30 seconds

The idea in one paragraph

AI may matter not mainly because it can answer questions faster, but because it can take bodies of information too large and complex for any individual to understand, make their structure visible, and help humans make better judgements about them. That could apply not only to science and organisations, but eventually to society itself. Computers have already transformed human life. The question is whether the next transformation simply happens to us, or whether AI can help humans understand it well enough to shape it deliberately.

About 3 minutes

The argument in seven steps

1. Important words often hide disagreement.

Words such as consciousness, intelligence, fairness and happiness feel precise until people have to agree exactly what they mean. The conversation began with a simple proposal: where a common word is carrying important scientific or social weight, unpack it into an explicit set of features, relationships and boundaries rather than allowing the word itself to do the work.

2. Meaning depends on what is available to the thinker.

We used the term GivenSpace for the information, experience, assumptions, definitions, memories and associations available when someone thinks about something. Two people can use the same word and mean different things because the word occupies a different place in each person's wider web of knowledge. Conversation works partly by enlarging the space that is shared.

3. Consciousness became the test case.

Instead of asking whether a system simply “is conscious”, science could first agree what belongs inside the concept and which features are essential. That would separate the definition of the phenomenon from the mechanism that implements it. The analogy discussed was flight: an aircraft does not need feathers to fly. A future artificial form of consciousness, if it exists, need not reproduce a biological brain in order to satisfy an agreed definition.

4. The same problem appears in AI itself.

Terms such as “intelligence” and “general intelligence” are also ordinary-language labels doing technical work. A more useful question is often not “Is this AGI?” but “Which capabilities are present, in which domains, at what level, and by what evidence?”

5. This led to AI judgement.

Humans already make decisions by compressing huge, incomplete bodies of evidence into an actionable conclusion. Law and medicine are obvious examples. AI may make some forms of complex judgement more inspectable and repeatable: the same evidence can be organised, the assumptions exposed, the method rerun, and different models compared. Agreement would not prove a judgement correct, but disagreement would reveal where it is unstable.

6. Computers have already changed society far more deeply than we usually notice.

The conversation identified several earlier changes: the creation of a vast virtual world; widespread trust in computer-maintained representations of money, identity and reality; software directing human work; and organisations becoming computationally optimisable systems. AI therefore does not introduce computers into society for the first time. It arrives inside a society already profoundly shaped by them.

7. The next question is whether complexity itself becomes more governable.

If AI can make very large systems comprehensible enough to judge, humans may gain a new tool for examining organisations, public decisions and eventually societal progress. The aim is not to hand moral authority to a machine. Humans choose the purposes and values. AI helps make the consequences, trade-offs and possible trajectories easier to see.

The choice may not be human agency versus AI interference. It may be human agency augmented by AI versus humans nominally controlling systems too complex for any individual to understand.

Words, sets and meaning

The starting insight was methodological: ordinary language evolved for flexible communication, not exact scientific specification. A familiar word can contain several overlapping meanings, change with context and carry assumptions that are invisible to the people using it.

A “set” in this conversation therefore means a deliberately explicit representation of a complex concept: its features, relationships, boundaries and, where useful, an essential subset. The common word remains as the label; the explicit structure underneath becomes load-bearing.

GivenSpace extends that idea. Meaning is not simply located inside a dictionary entry. It arises from the position a concept occupies among everything else available to the thinker. Clarification is therefore not merely choosing better wording; it is increasing the amount of relevant meaning that two minds share.

Consciousness as an example

The conversation explored whether consciousness research could first agree on the object it is trying to explain. Instead of rival theories quietly using the same word differently, the field could identify candidate components, decide which belong in the wider concept, and attempt to agree an essential subset.

This produces a different question about artificial consciousness. Biology gives us the only implementation currently known. But implementation and definition need not be identical. Bird flight and aircraft flight use radically different mechanisms while satisfying the higher-level phenomenon of flight. The same logic could, in principle, apply to a non-biological implementation of whatever science eventually decides consciousness means.

From objective measurement to difficult judgement

Academic and professional work lies on a broad continuum. Some conclusions are tightly determined by formal rules or measurement; others require judgement under uncertainty. Yet subjective judgement does not mean arbitrary judgement.

The interesting possibility is reproducible subjective judgement. An AI can be given the same evidence and explicit procedure repeatedly. Another AI can be given the same task. Humans can inspect where they converge and where they diverge. The result remains contestable, but the process becomes easier to expose and challenge than much private human judgement.

This is one reason the conversation kept returning to compression: taking a large, messy and uncertain information space and reducing it to something a human can actually act upon without pretending the uncertainty disappeared.

Organisations as a practical experiment

A practical line of work asks whether AI can use an organisation's own public material to identify what it says it is for, what values it claims, and what evidence it provides about delivery. A related experiment asks whether AI can first infer a defensible framework for societal value from the wider public record, then organise company evidence against that framework.

The important point is not that AI possesses its own morality. It is that AI may be able to synthesise values humans have already expressed across laws, standards, institutions and public discourse, turn them into a transparent framework, and then apply that framework consistently enough for humans to inspect the result.

Four changes computers have already made

The conversation proposed a deliberately high-level history. First, computation helped create an effectively unlimited virtual world in which people now spend large parts of their lives. Second, society came to trust computer-maintained records as authoritative representations of money, identity, ownership and many other facts. Third, software began to direct and evaluate human activity. Fourth, entire organisations became systems that could model and optimise themselves computationally.

This reframes arguments about whether computers should “interfere” with human society. They already do, at enormous depth. AI arrives after that first transformation, not before it.

A possible second transformation

The proposed next change is not simply more automation. It is the possibility that AI makes previously unmanageable complexity intelligible enough for humans to judge. That could increase human agency if it helps decision-makers understand systems that computation itself helped make too complex for unaided human oversight.

The distinction matters. One future uses AI mainly to optimise inherited systems faster. Another also uses it to question whether the purposes, measures and structures of those systems still make sense. The conversation called this the difference between optimising the world we inherited and helping humanity design what comes next.

Society, progress and happiness

If the method works for organisations, it may extend to society. Instead of asking only whether a country is richer than before, AI could help compare actual progress with plausible alternative trajectories, identify what is driving change and show where apparent progress may simply reflect technological tailwinds rather than good institutional design.

The conversation eventually reached a deliberately simple candidate for society's highest-level purpose: help humans live happy lives. But “happy” immediately becomes another load-bearing word, so it too must be unpacked. The resulting set could include security, health, belonging, autonomy, meaning, purpose, fairness, opportunity, hope, relationships and sufficient material resources, among other dimensions.

That suggests a possible feedback loop: define what matters, measure movement, diagnose causes, propose interventions, let humans decide, act, and measure again. Feedback becomes powerful when coupled to intelligent design.

The question left at the end

AI-native society is likely to emerge through countless decisions rather than one grand plan. The conversation's final challenge was therefore directed less at a particular technology than at the mindset behind its use.

Are we building intelligence mainly to optimise the systems of the last millennium, or intelligence that also helps humanity reconsider the systems themselves?

The conversation did not claim to answer that question. It argued that the capability is important enough to test.
