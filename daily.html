A short record of what changed in the thinking today.

Today was largely about one question: what might AI make possible that was not possible before?

We began with something deceptively simple: words. Words such as consciousness, intelligence, fairness and happiness carry enormous conceptual weight, yet their meanings are often assumed rather than explicitly defined. One approach we have been exploring is to treat such concepts as sets: structured collections of features and relationships that make the meaning behind a word more explicit.

That led to a broader question about judgement. Some human judgements are relatively objective; others require weighing enormous amounts of incomplete evidence, competing interpretations and values. Law and medicine provide obvious examples. Humans routinely have to compress very complex situations into decisions.

AI may introduce something new here. It can process bodies of information too large for any individual human to comprehend, structure that information, expose assumptions and competing interpretations, and present the result in a form humans can use to make decisions.

This does not require believing that AI is always right. AI systems have biases, limitations and opaque internal processes. But their inputs, frameworks and outputs can often be made explicit, compared and tested — including across different AI models.

We then placed this capability in a longer historical context. Over recent decades, computers have created a virtual world in which humans increasingly live and work; become trusted custodians of important representations of reality; begun directing human activity; and enabled organisations to optimise themselves computationally.

AI may represent the next stage: helping humans make an increasingly complex world understandable enough to judge.

That raises a still larger possibility. Could AI eventually help societies understand how well they are progressing, identify where improvement is possible, and provide decision-makers with better options?

One simple example emerged late today. If the purpose of society is ultimately to help humans live happy lives, “happiness” itself can be treated as a set rather than a vague word. AI could potentially monitor those dimensions over time, report whether they are improving or declining, identify likely causes, and help triage possible responses. Humans would still decide what to do.

The next major societal transition involving computers may happen whether or not anyone deliberately designs it. The interesting question is whether, this time, we can help shape its direction.

Elsewhere today we explored a historic automotive idea, accidentally discovered an entertaining comparison involving luxury branding and condoms, developed an experiment around AI analysis of major public decisions, progressed several organisational projects, and decided that this growing collection of work probably needs a website of its own.
